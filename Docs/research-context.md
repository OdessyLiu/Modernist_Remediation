# Research Context

## Project Overview

This project investigates **readerly expectations of graphic biographies** through Goodreads reviews.

Graphic biography sits at the intersection of biography, comics, visual narrative, and life writing. While much scholarship on autobiographical and literary comics emphasizes intimacy, confession, trauma, subjectivity, and formal experimentation, graphic biographies often represent historically distant or publicly known figures through more externally oriented narrative forms. This makes graphic biography a useful case for examining how readers recognize, evaluate, and negotiate an emerging or unstable genre.

Rather than defining graphic biography only through textual features, this study approaches genre as partly constituted through **reader reception and expectation**.

## Research Objectives

The project has two primary objectives:

1. **Describe the readerly expectations associated with graphic biographies.**
   - What aspects of graphic biographies do readers notice, value, criticize, or expect?
   - What recurring evaluative criteria appear across Goodreads reviews?
   - Where do readers articulate fulfilled, violated, or competing expectations?

2. **Explore computational methods for studying evaluative and expectation-related language in online reader communities.**
   - Identify methods that can surface recurring evaluative concerns without reducing interpretation to sentiment scores alone.
   - Combine quantitative corpus analysis with contextual reading of review language.
   - Evaluate how computational methods can support interpretive literary and cultural analysis.

The project is exploratory. It aims to identify patterns and categories that can support a theory of readerly expectation rather than to produce a fixed or exhaustive taxonomy of graphic biography.

## Theoretical Orientation

The project draws on a conception of genre as involving multiple interacting dimensions, including:

- literary production practices;
- textual conventions or templates;
- readerly expectations.

This study concentrates primarily on **readerly expectation**.

Readerly expectation refers to the assumptions, norms, desires, and evaluative standards that readers bring to texts they recognize as belonging to, or approximating, a genre. These expectations can become visible when readers describe:

- what a work should have done;
- what they expected before or during reading;
- what they wanted more or less of;
- what felt appropriate or inappropriate;
- what succeeded or failed according to perceived genre norms.

Readerly expectation is therefore related to evaluation but is not identical to it.

## Evaluation, Valuation, and Expectation

The project distinguishes among three related concepts:

### Evaluation

Evaluation refers to explicit or implicit judgments about the quality, effectiveness, value, or success of a book.

Examples include judgments that a book is:

- engaging;
- shallow;
- informative;
- beautiful;
- confusing;
- well researched;
- emotionally effective.

Evaluation may be expressed linguistically, through star ratings, or through other forms of reader response.

### Valence

Valence refers more narrowly to the positive or negative orientation of evaluative language.

A review can contain strongly negative valence without clearly revealing the underlying expectation. For example, describing a biography as "too superficial" is negative, but the analytically important issue is not only negativity: it also implies an expectation for greater depth.

For this reason, sentiment or valence is treated as one analytical signal rather than as a substitute for evaluation or expectation.

### Readerly Expectation

Readerly expectation concerns the criteria or assumptions against which readers evaluate a work.

Statements such as:

- "I wanted more about her personal life";
- "this should have gone into greater depth";
- "I expected more historical context";
- "the artwork was too distracting";
- "this works well as an introduction";

reveal not only whether a reader liked the book, but also what they believe a graphic biography ought to provide.

The analytical goal is therefore to move from **whether readers evaluate positively or negatively** toward **what they appear to be evaluating and according to which expectations**.

## Goodreads as a Reception Corpus

Goodreads reviews are treated as evidence of **publicly articulated reader reception**.

They are useful because they provide:

- large quantities of reader-generated evaluative language;
- review-level star ratings;
- naturally occurring commentary on reading experiences;
- disagreement among readers;
- comparisons between expectations and perceived outcomes;
- discussion of both narrative and visual dimensions of books.

However, Goodreads users are not representative of all readers, and Goodreads reviews should not be treated as transparent records of private reading experience.

The corpus represents a platform-specific discourse shaped by:

- self-selection;
- reviewing practices;
- Goodreads interface conventions;
- rating culture;
- social interaction;
- differing reviewer activity levels.

Claims should therefore be framed as patterns within the collected Goodreads review corpus rather than universal claims about all readers of graphic biography.

## Why Computational Analysis

The corpus is large enough that close reading alone cannot reliably characterize its dominant and recurring patterns.

Computational analysis is used to:

- describe the composition of the corpus;
- identify lexical patterns that recur across many reviews;
- compare differently evaluated subsets of the corpus;
- locate language associated with reader expectations;
- identify candidate patterns for closer qualitative interpretation.

The computational methods are not intended to replace interpretation.

The project follows a **corpus-assisted interpretive workflow**:

> aggregate pattern → candidate feature → contextual inspection → interpretive category

Quantitative results are therefore treated as evidence that directs and constrains qualitative interpretation rather than as self-sufficient explanations.

## Key Analytical Interest

A central question throughout the project is:

> **What do readers appear to believe a graphic biography should do?**

Potential expectation domains may include, but are not limited to:

- biographical depth;
- historical context;
- factual or educational value;
- characterization of the biographical subject;
- emotional engagement;
- narrative coherence;
- pacing;
- accessibility;
- visual style;
- relationships between image and text;
- completeness or selectivity;
- originality;
- suitability as an introduction to a person or historical subject.

These should not be treated as predetermined coding categories. Final domains should emerge from corpus evidence and contextual interpretation.

## Scope of Claims

This project can make claims about:

- recurring evaluative language in the Goodreads corpus;
- reader expectations articulated or implied in reviews;
- relationships between evaluation and particular topics or criteria;
- differences between more positively and negatively evaluated reviews;
- how computational corpus methods can support reception-oriented genre research.

It should not, on Goodreads evidence alone, make strong claims about:

- authorial intention;
- publishing or production practices;
- formal conventions across the entire graphic-biography genre;
- the attitudes of readers who do not review books online;
- causal relationships between textual features and reader reactions.

These distinctions should remain explicit throughout analysis and interpretation.
